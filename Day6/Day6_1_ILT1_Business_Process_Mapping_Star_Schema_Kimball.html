What Is a Business Process?

A repeatable, measurable activity

Picture Sushant Kumar's manager stopping by his desk: "Can you build me something for sales performance?" Sushant doesn't touch a keyboard yet — "sales performance" is too vague to build anything from. There's no single event hiding inside that phrase. So he asks a follow-up: what exact event do you want counted, every time it happens? The real answer: a sale happening — a customer buying N units of a product, at a price, on a date. That's something you can point at, count, and track over time, forever.

That's the whole idea behind the term business process: something that happens in the business over and over, in a way you can count, sum, or track over time. "A sale happening" is a business process. "Sales performance" is not. That's what gets modeled.

Imagine building a brand-new table every time someone asks for a report — sales by region, sales by month, sales by rep. You'd end up with the same numbers copied into a dozen different tables, and no way to know which copy is right when two of them disagree. That's exactly the mess a warehouse exists to prevent.

So instead, build the underlying business process once — the sale itself — as one trustworthy table: fact_sales. Every report after that is just a different GROUP BY on that same table, not a new copy of the data. This is Kimball's core rule: model the warehouse around business processes, not around report requests.

Facts vs. Dimensions

One Sentence, Two Kinds of Words

Say Sushant Kumar buys a keyboard, today. Split that sentence into two kinds of pieces:

  • The action — "buys." This is the thing that actually happened, the event you'd want to count or track. In a warehouse, this piece lives in what's called a fact table.
  • Everything else — "Sushant," "keyboard," "today." These don't describe an event on their own; they describe the who, what, and when around it. In a warehouse, these pieces live in what are called dimension tables.

Kimball has a one-liner for this split: facts are verbs, dimensions are nouns.

Same Pattern, a Real Sale

A sale happens. That's the fact.

  • to a customer
  • of a product
  • on a date
  • shipped to an address
  • paid by a method
  • as part of an order

Six nouns. Six dimensions.

Neither Is Useful Alone

fact_sales with no dimensions is a pile of numbers — "₹14,392,000 in Sales_amount" means nothing without knowing whose sales, which products, when.

dim_customer with no fact table is just a phone book.

The value is in the join.

  • products
  • customers

The star schema fixes exactly that problem. It collapses those 7 tables into one fact table (the sales events) surrounded by the dimensions that describe them — so a question that used to take 7 joins now takes 1.

Notice this list has six tables, not five — the original Day 1 sketch only had five dimensions. Why the extra one? Some details, like which channel the order came through, the shipping tier, or which supplier fulfilled it, describe the order as a whole — not the customer, not the product, not the date. They don't fit cleanly into any of the other five nouns, so they get their own table: dim_orders. That's the Day 1 spec's five, plus this one addition. ILT 2 (next) designs each of these six in depth.

Two of These Six Are Special

People and products change. A customer updates their phone number. A product's price goes up next month.

Picture what would happen if we just overwrote the old phone number with the new one: every past order that customer ever placed would now show today's phone number attached to it — even though that number didn't exist yet when the order actually happened. That's wrong. And once overwritten, the old number is gone for good; there's no way to get it back.

So instead of overwriting, dim_customer and dim_product keep the OLD row exactly as it was, and add a brand NEW row with the new information. Both stay on file: what was true before, and what's true now.

This pattern — keep old rows, add new ones instead of overwriting — has a name you'll hear a lot starting in ILT 2: SCD Type 2 (SCD2).

Quick-Fire Q&A

Business Processes & the Star Shape

Click a question to reveal the answer

We modeled around "business processes" instead of departments. What's the actual reason?
Business processes (place an order, ship an order, process a return) stay stable and are shared across departments. Org charts and report requests do not — they change constantly. Modeling around the process means the same fact table serves Sales, Finance, and Ops without a redesign every time someone in one department wants a new report.
In one sentence — what's the difference between a fact and a dimension?
A fact is a measured event — something that happened, with numbers attached. A dimension is the context around that event — who, what, when, where. fact_sales = "an order line item happened"; dim_customer/dim_product/dim_date = who bought it, what it was, when.
Is a star schema the same thing as a normalized (3NF) database design?
No — the opposite, on purpose. A normalized (3NF) design splits data into many small tables to avoid repeating anything — great for a system taking fast, one-row-at-a-time writes. A star schema does the reverse: it keeps each dimension as one flat table, even if that repeats some data, because that's what makes reading and reporting fast — one join per dimension, no chains of joins.
Why "star" and not "snowflake" in this course?
Snowflake normalizes dimensions further (e.g. splitting dim_product into product + category + subcategory tables) to save storage. Star keeps each dimension flat in one table. Storage is cheap; joins are the cost that matters for a BI/Genie query — so this course keeps every dimension flat, one join away.
Quick-Fire Q&A

Reading the Whiteboard Sketch

Click a question to reveal the answer

The whiteboard sketch has 8 boxes around fact_sales, but this course only builds 6 dimensions. What happened to Order_items_Dimension and Returns_Dimension?
Good eye — Sushant Kumar and Aman Thapliyal counted the same 8 boxes on the whiteboard and stopped mid-sketch to double-check. They're reference-only in this design — not foreign keys on fact_sales. Order items are fact_sales itself (that's the grain); returns will get its own fact_returns table on a later day, built against the very same dim_customer/dim_product tables rather than its own copies — that reuse is what's meant by "conformed": both fact tables agree on who the customers and products are, so a return and the sale it's tied to always line up. They're related concepts on the sketch, not additional joins to build today.
Does every business process at GlobalMart need its own fact table?
Only the ones worth measuring on their own grain. "Place an order" and "process a return" are separate processes with separate grains — that's two fact tables. A process like "update a customer's phone number" isn't measured at all; it's just a dimension attribute change, no fact table needed.
What comes first when designing a star schema — picking the dimensions or declaring the grain?
Grain, always — Kimball's four-step method is business process → grain → dimensions → facts, in that order. Think of it like Sushant Kumar organizing lunch for the cohort: nobody picks a restaurant until the group agrees on "lunch, today, for six people." Skip that step and the whole plan falls apart. Same with a star schema — pick dimensions before grain, and you end up with the wrong grain by accident.
If fact_sales is the "star," what's the actual shape that name refers to?
Literally star-shaped when diagrammed — one central fact table with dimension tables radiating outward as single points, each one join away. No dimension joins another dimension directly; that's what keeps the shape a star instead of a chain or snowflake.
