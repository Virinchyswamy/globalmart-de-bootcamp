What Is a Business Process?

A repeatable, measurable activity

A business process is a repeatable, measurable activity that happens in the business — something that generates events you can count, sum, or track over time.

"Sales performance" is not a business process — too vague. A sale happening — a customer buying N units of a product, at a price, on a date — is.

Kimball's core rule: build the warehouse one business process at a time, not one report at a time.

Model the report every team asks for as a fresh table, and you get GlobalMart's original Data Silos and Unclear Origin problems all over again. Model the sale process once, correctly, as fact_sales — every report becomes a different GROUP BY on the same trusted table.

Business Process — or Not?

Not a business processA business process
"Sales performance" — vague, no clear eventA sale happening — customer buys product(s), in an order, on a date
"Customer satisfaction" — an opinionA return being filed — a measurable event with date, product, reason
"Marketing effectiveness" — a judgmentA campaign being sent and opened — a measurable event

Facts vs. Dimensions

Kimball's one-liner

Facts are verbs, dimensions are nouns.

A sale happens (verb, fact) to a customer, of a product, on a date, shipped to an address, paid by a method, as part of an order (all nouns, dimensions).

Fact vs. Dimension — Side by Side

FactDimension
What it representsA measurable business eventContext describing that event
ContainsNumbers to SUM/AVG/COUNT + foreign keysDescriptive attributes to filter/group by
GrowsFast — one row per eventSlowly — a customer doesn't change daily
GlobalMart examplefact_sales — one row per order line itemdim_customer, dim_product, dim_date, dim_address, dim_payment_method, dim_orders

Neither Is Useful Alone

fact_sales with no dimensions is a pile of numbers — "₹14,392,000 in Sales_amount" means nothing without knowing whose sales, which products, when.

dim_customer with no fact table is just a phone book.

The value is in the join.

Applying It

GlobalMart's Business Process

Every ingestion pipeline, every Bronze/Silver table, every CDC and Autoloader job built so far exists to make one process measurable correctly.

A Customer Buys Product(s) In an Order

GlobalMart's core business process

That data is clean in Silver today, but scattered across 7 tables shaped like the source systems:

  • orders
  • order_items
  • products
  • customers
  • addresses
  • payments
  • payment_methods

The star schema's job: collapse those 7 tables into one fact table surrounded by the dimensions that give it meaning.

fact_sales's grain, measures (Quantity_purchased, Actual_price, Discounted_price, Sales_amount), and 6 dimensions were already named back on Day 1 as this course's central deliverable. Today's job is to understand why that shape is the right one — grain and dimension design get their full rigorous treatment in ILT 2, right after this session.

The Shape

The Star Schema Shape

One fact table in the middle. Six dimensions around it, each answering a different question about the sale.

What Each Table Does

TableRoleGrain / Contents
fact_salesCenter of the starOne row per order line item
dim_customerWho bought itOne row per customer version (SCD2)
dim_productWhat was soldOne row per product version (SCD2)
dim_dateWhenOne row per calendar day — generated, not sourced from Silver
dim_addressWhere it shippedOne row per address
dim_payment_methodHow it was paidOne row per payment method (~5 rows total)
dim_ordersOrder header contextOne row per order (channel, shipping tier, supplier)

Six dimensions — matching the Day 1 spec's spirit, with one addition: dim_orders for order-header attributes. ILT 2 (next) designs each one in depth. Two of them — dim_customer and dim_product — already carry SCD2 history by the time you get to them, because Silver (Day 5) built it in; today's hands-on republishes it in Gold, it doesn't build it from scratch.
