The Scenario ⏱ 5 min

GlobalMart Warehouse Ops Needs an Inventory Star Schema

GlobalMart's warehouse operations team runs five distribution centers across India. Every night, at midnight, an automated job walks every product on every warehouse's shelves and counts how many units are physically on hand. That nightly count gets exported as a raw file and dropped on your desk to process — picture Kumar Pratik opening his inbox tomorrow morning to find three fresh Excel files and a one-line request from warehouse ops, because that's exactly the seat you're sitting in for this activity. Warehouse ops wants a Gold-layer star schema built on top of this data so they can finally answer a question they've been asking for months:

The business question driving this build: "How many units of Product X do we have on hand across all our warehouses right now, and how has that changed over the last quarter?"

That single question is why the fact table exists — every design decision below (grain, what's additive, what isn't) traces back to being able to answer it correctly. You've been handed three raw export files and a starter workbook to build into. Open all three raw files now and skim them before moving to Phase 1 — don't hunt for problems yet, just get familiar with what each one contains. (The problems will find you soon enough.)

Phase 1 ⏱ 10 min

1Explore & Declare the Grain

Before you build a single dimension table, sit with the single most important question in fact table design: what does one row of fact_inventory_snapshot actually represent? Get this wrong now and you won't find out until Phase 6, staring at a PivotTable that makes no sense and wondering where it all went sideways — the dimension lookups, the PivotTable, the additive-measures question, all of it traces back to this one sentence.

  • Open Warehouses_Raw.xlsx, Products_Raw.xlsx, and NightlyCounts_Raw.xlsx. Actually scroll through them — don't just glance at row 1.
  • Look closely at NightlyCounts_Raw.xlsx in particular. For a single Warehouse_Code + Product_ID combination, how many rows exist across the file? What's different between those rows?
  • Ask yourself: does a row get created here only when something happens (like an order being placed), or does a row get created on a fixed schedule regardless of whether anything changed?
  • Write one sentence — the grain declaration — describing exactly what a single row of fact_inventory_snapshot represents. Use the same sentence structure you used this morning for fact_sales: "One row = one <what> for a given <which dimensions>."
  • Keep this sentence somewhere you'll reference it later — you'll need it for the Phase 7 checklist, and it will guide every decision in Phase 5.
    • Need a nudge?

    Look at what actually varies row to row in NightlyCounts_Raw.xlsx for one warehouse/product pair. It isn't quantity-per-transaction the way a sales order line is — there's no "order" happening here at all.

    Compare this to this morning's fact_sales: a sales fact row exists because a transaction occurred — no sale, no row. Does that same logic hold for NightlyCounts_Raw.xlsx? Or does a row get created every single night for every single warehouse/product pair, whether or not the count actually changed from the night before?

    That distinction — "a row exists because an event happened" vs. "a row exists because a clock ticked and we checked in" — is exactly the kind of fact table type distinction ILT3 covered this morning. Land on which one this is, and your grain sentence should fall out naturally.

    Checkpoint

    You should have a single, specific sentence written down before moving to Phase 2. If your sentence doesn't mention a warehouse, a product, and a date all in the same breath, it's not specific enough yet — go back and tighten it. Vague grain sentences have a way of coming back to haunt you exactly one phase later.

    Phase 2 ⏱ 10 min

    2Build dim_warehouse

    Five real warehouses live in Warehouses_Raw.xlsx — but the raw file has 6 rows and a few data quality issues layered on top. GlobalMart did not, in fact, open a sixth distribution center overnight. Work through the issues in order.

  • Open Warehouses_Raw.xlsx and sort by Warehouse_Code (Data tab → Sort A→Z). Look at every row carefully.
  • Check WH01 — you'll find two rows for it. Same warehouse, same code, same city, but the Capacity_Units value differs between the two rows. There's no timestamp column to tell you which row is "newer" or more authoritative.
  • Decide which Capacity_Units value to trust for WH01, and write a one-line note documenting your decision and your reasoning — literally type it into a spare cell or a text box in the workbook. There is no way to know for certain which value is correct from the data alone; that's the point. In real data-quality work, when two rows genuinely conflict and you can't arbitrate with a timestamp or a second source, the right move is to make your best independent judgment call (e.g., cross-check against the warehouse's known scale, or simply pick the value that looks more complete/plausible) and document it openly — so anyone downstream can see the assumption and challenge it later if needed. Don't just silently pick one and move on.
  • Fix the inconsistent city casing. One row's City is all-lowercase, another is ALL-CAPS. Use =PROPER(cell) in a helper column to standardize casing across the whole column, then paste the results back as values.
  • Find the warehouse with a blank Region cell and fill it in. With only 5 warehouses in the table, you can resolve this with a quick manual look — check the warehouse's city and fill in the region that matches the pattern of the other rows.
  • Once you have exactly 5 clean, deduplicated rows with consistent casing and no blanks, copy them into the dim_warehouse tab of Starter_Workbook.xlsx.
  • Assign a warehouse_sk to each row: a plain sequential integer, 1 through 5, typed down the column (or generated with =ROW()-1 if your table starts on row 2). This is your surrogate key.

    • Excel-native surrogate keys, vs. this morning's sha2(). In Databricks this morning, you generated surrogate keys with sha2(natural_key, 256) — a hash of the natural key, deterministic and collision-resistant at scale. Excel has no equivalent hash function suited to this, and no, you don't need to hand-type a SHA-256 hash to feel like a real data engineer. The Excel-native way to get the same concept — an ID that the dimension table owns, that's meaningless outside that table, that exists purely as a stable join target — is simpler: after the table is fully deduplicated and cleaned, just number the rows sequentially, 1, 2, 3... The mechanism changed because the tool changed. The concept — a stable, table-owned surrogate identifier used only for joining — did not.

    Checkpoint

    You should have exactly 5 rows in dim_warehouse, each with a unique warehouse_sk from 1–5, consistent title-case city names, and no blank cells anywhere. If you have 6 rows, you still have the WH01 duplicate sitting in there — go back to step 2. It won't resolve itself.

    Phase 3 ⏱ 10 min

    3Build dim_product

    Products_Raw.xlsx has 39 rows behind 34 real products — 5 rows too many, from two different kinds of duplication. Handle them one at a time; don't try to spot both at once, or you'll end up chasing two different ghosts simultaneously.

  • Open Products_Raw.xlsx. First pass: remove the easy duplicates. Select the full data range, go to Data tab → Remove Duplicates, and check all columns. This handles rows that are exact duplicates — identical in every single column, a re-export artifact. This should remove 3 rows.
  • Second pass: after the exact duplicates are gone, sort or filter by Product_ID (Data tab → Sort, or turn on AutoFilter). Look for any Product_ID that still appears twice. Remove Duplicates won't catch these — they aren't identical rows.
  • For each repeated Product_ID you find, compare the two rows column by column. You're looking for a case-sensitivity mismatch — the same category spelled with different capitalization between the two occurrences (e.g. one row says the category in Proper Case, the other in all-lowercase). Since they refer to the same real product, treat them as one product: keep the properly-cased version and delete the other row.
  • Once resolved, you should have 34 unique products left, one row per Product_ID.
  • Copy the 34 clean rows into the dim_product tab of Starter_Workbook.xlsx.
  • Assign product_sk as a sequential integer, 1 through 34, exactly the same approach as warehouse_sk in Phase 2.
    • Need a nudge on finding the near-duplicates?

    After removing the 3 exact duplicates, sort the whole table by Product_ID ascending. A true near-duplicate will show up as two rows sitting right next to each other with the same Product_ID — everything else about them will look almost identical except for the casing in one column. Scan down the sorted list for any Product_ID you see twice.

    An alternative: use conditional formatting (Home tab → Conditional Formatting → Highlight Cell Rules → Duplicate Values) on just the Product_ID column to visually flag any code that still appears more than once.

    Checkpoint

    You should have exactly 34 rows in dim_product, each with a unique Product_ID and consistent casing throughout. If you have 35 or 36, one of the near-duplicate pairs is still hiding in the table — go find it before it finds you in Phase 5's lookups.

    Phase 4 ⏱ 5–8 min

    4Build dim_date

    Unlike the other two dimensions, dim_date isn't cleaned from a raw file — it's generated, which makes this the closest thing to a breather you'll get in this activity. The observed date range across the nightly counts is June 1–10, 2026 (10 nights), so this dimension needs exactly 10 rows, one per calendar day in that range. Enjoy it — Phase 5 is next.

  • In the dim_date tab of Starter_Workbook.xlsx, fill the date column with 10 consecutive real Excel dates: 6/1/2026 through 6/10/2026. Type the first date, then use Fill → Series (or drag the fill handle) to generate the rest — don't type all 10 by hand.
  • Build date_key as a readable integer: =VALUE(TEXT(date,"YYYYMMDD")) (the TEXT() produces a string like "20260601"; wrapping it in VALUE() converts that string into a true number). Paste the results as values once done.
  • Build day_of_week with =TEXT(date,"dddd") — this returns the full weekday name (e.g. "Monday").
  • Build month with =MONTH(date).
  • Build quarter with =ROUNDUP(MONTH(date)/3,0).
  • Build year with =YEAR(date).
  • Convert every formula column to static values (copy → Paste Special → Values) once you've confirmed all 10 rows look correct.

    • Why date_key isn't a sequential integer. Every other surrogate key in this activity (warehouse_sk, product_sk) is a meaningless sequential number. date_key is the deliberate exception, and it was the exception this morning too: a human-readable YYYYMMDD integer sorts correctly, is instantly recognizable in a filter or a chart axis, and lets you range-filter (date_key BETWEEN 20260601 AND 20260610) without ever joining back to the date table. Same reasoning ILT2 gave this morning for why date dimensions get the one exception to "surrogate keys should be meaningless."

    Checkpoint

    dim_date should have exactly 10 rows — June 1 through June 10, 2026 — with all six columns populated and no formulas left live (values only). Ten rows, six columns, zero drama — savor it.

    Phase 5 — The Core of This Activity ⏱ 20 min

    5Build fact_inventory_snapshot

    This is where all three finished dimensions finally get put to work — and where the activity earns its "core" label. NightlyCounts_Raw.xlsx has 1,742 rows, and it carries three distinct data-quality problems stacked on top of each other. Work through them in the exact order below — don't skip ahead to the lookups before the raw data is actually clean, however tempting that shortcut looks.

  • Stage the raw data. Open NightlyCounts_Raw.xlsx and copy all 1,742 rows into a new, blank scratch sheet inside Starter_Workbook.xlsx (not directly into the fact_inventory_snapshot tab — you need a working area to clean in before the final paste).
  • Remove exact duplicates. Select the full range on your scratch sheet, go to Data tab → Remove Duplicates, and check all 5 columns (Count_Batch_ID, Warehouse_Code, Product_ID, Count_Date, Units_On_Hand). This is a system export glitch — the same warehouse+product+date+count repeated verbatim. This step should remove 20 rows.
  • Normalize Count_Date. This column mixes three different formats in the same column: real Excel date values, US-style text like 06/10/2026, and long text like June 7, 2026. Add a helper column with:

    =IF(ISNUMBER(Count_Date), Count_Date, DATEVALUE(Count_Date))

    ISNUMBER() is the key check here: a cell that's already a real Excel date stores internally as a number, and wrapping an already-real date in DATEVALUE() will throw an error — so the formula only calls DATEVALUE() on the rows that actually need text-to-date conversion. Once the helper column looks correct for all rows, paste it as values and replace the original Count_Date column.
  • ⚠️ Check your locale before trusting DATEVALUE() on the slash-format rows. DATEVALUE("06/10/2026") was written as a US-style MM/DD/YYYY string (June 10), but DATEVALUE()'s parsing depends on your Excel's regional date setting, not the string's intent. On a day-first regional setting (DD/MM/YYYY — common on Excel installs configured for India), Excel will silently read "06/10/2026" as 10 June reversed to day=6, month=10 → 6 October — no error, just a wrong date. Spot-check it: after building the helper column, filter it and confirm every resulting date's month is June. If you find dates outside June, your Excel is parsing day-first, and the plain DATEVALUE() formula from the previous step isn't safe.

    If that's the case, replace the whole helper-column formula with this three-way version instead of just patching the slash case — a single flat DATEVALUE() replacement would fix the slash rows but silently break the long-text rows in the process (LEFT("June 7, 2026", 2) isn't a number, so a naive fixed-position formula applied to every row would turn working rows into new errors):

    =IF(ISNUMBER(Count_Date), Count_Date, IF(ISNUMBER(SEARCH("/",Count_Date)), DATE(VALUE(RIGHT(Count_Date,4)), VALUE(LEFT(Count_Date,2)), VALUE(MID(Count_Date,4,2))), DATEVALUE(Count_Date)))

    Read it as three branches: already a real date → keep it; contains a / → pull year/month/day out of the known MM/DD/YYYY positions explicitly, ignoring Excel's locale guess entirely; otherwise (the long-text rows like "June 7, 2026") → DATEVALUE() is safe here, a month name has no day/month ambiguity to misparse.
  • Find and exclude the blank-warehouse rows. Use =COUNTBLANK() on the Warehouse_Code column, or turn on AutoFilter and filter that column to blanks, to locate them. You'll find 12 rows with no Warehouse_Code at all. Delete these rows entirely — do not guess which warehouse they belong to. There is nothing else in the row that reveals which warehouse it came from, and a fact row that can't be safely joined to dim_warehouse is a fact row that shouldn't exist in the final table. This is exactly the referential-integrity principle from this morning's Silver-layer work: if you can't join it safely, you quarantine it rather than fabricate a value.
  • Find and exclude the negative-count rows. Filter Units_On_Hand for values less than 0. You'll find 10 rows with a negative unit count. Delete these rows too. A warehouse cannot physically hold −23 units of anything — this is a data-entry error, not a valid measurement, and a real data engineer excludes physically impossible values rather than passing them downstream.
  • Look up warehouse_sk. Against your finished dim_warehouse tab, use =XLOOKUP(Warehouse_Code, dim_warehouse!Warehouse_Code_column, dim_warehouse!warehouse_sk_column) (or =VLOOKUP(Warehouse_Code, dim_warehouse!A:F, [warehouse_sk column number], FALSE) if you're on an Excel version without XLOOKUP) for every remaining row.
  • Look up product_sk. Same approach, against your finished dim_product tab, matching on Product_ID.
  • Look up date_key. Against your finished dim_date tab, matching your normalized Count_Date value to the date column, returning date_key. If a large chunk of rows come back #N/A here (not just one or two), don't start debugging the lookup formula itself first — go back to the locale check two steps ago. A systematic block of #N/As is the signature of dates that got parsed into the wrong month, not a broken XLOOKUP.
  • Leave count_batch_id exactly as-is — no lookup. Notice this column doesn't get resolved against any dimension table at all, unlike the other three keys. That's deliberate: count_batch_id is a degenerate dimension. It's an identifier that lives directly on the fact row (one batch per night, shared by every warehouse/product row counted that night) but has no dimension table of its own — there's nothing to describe about a batch beyond its own ID, so it never gets a surrogate key or a lookup, just like Order_ID in this morning's ILT3 pattern, except here there's truly no competing dimension trying to claim the label.
  • Paste the final 5 columns as values — warehouse_sk, product_sk, date_key, count_batch_id, units_on_hand — into the real fact_inventory_snapshot tab of Starter_Workbook.xlsx. Use Paste Special → Values so no formulas or lookups remain live.
    • Checkpoint — the single most important number in this activity

    Your finished fact_inventory_snapshot tab should have exactly 1,700 rows. Not 1,699, not 1,701 — exactly 1,700.

    Work backward if it doesn't match: 1,742 raw rows − 20 exact duplicates − 12 blank-warehouse rows − 10 negative-units rows = 1,700. If your count is off, you either kept a duplicate you should have removed, deleted a valid row by mistake, or missed one of the two exclusion filters. Don't move on to Phase 6 until this number is exactly 1,700.

    Phase 6 ⏱ 10 min

    6Verify With a PivotTable

    A finished fact table isn't proven correct just because the row count matches — 1,700 is necessary, not sufficient. This phase makes the additive vs. semi-additive distinction from this morning's ILT3 concrete, using your own data instead of a slide.

    Step 1 — Build the PivotTable

  • Click anywhere inside your finished fact_inventory_snapshot tab.
  • Go to Insert tab → PivotTable, and place it on a new worksheet.
  • Drag warehouse_sk and product_sk into Rows, date_key into Columns, and units_on_hand into Values, set to Sum.

    • Step 2 — Confirm a safe additive summary

    Remove date_key from Columns for a moment, and just look at total units_on_hand grouped by warehouse_sk, or grouped by product_sk, for a single date (filter date_key down to one value first). Confirm the sum looks sensible — e.g. total units of a product across all 5 warehouses on one given night is a real, meaningful number. Summing across warehouses for a fixed point in time is safe.

    Step 3 — Break it on purpose

    Now do the opposite: pick one single warehouse_sk + product_sk combination, and instead sum units_on_hand across all 10 dates for that one combination. Look at the total the PivotTable gives you.

    That total is meaningless — and that's the point. Units_On_Hand is a snapshot balance, not a transaction amount. Summing it across 10 nights doesn't give you "10 nights of activity" the way summing Sales across 10 days would — it gives you the same physical stock counted, and added to itself, 10 separate times. GlobalMart does not have ten times as much stock as it thinks it does; your PivotTable just lied to you politely. This is the semi-additive measure problem from ILT3, made real: safe to sum across warehouses or products at a fixed point in time, meaningless to sum across time itself.

  • In your workbook (a spare cell, sticky note, or text box near the PivotTable), write one sentence explaining why summing units_on_hand across all 10 dates for one warehouse/product is wrong.
  • In the same note, state what the correct way to look at this measure over time would be instead — think about what ILT3 taught for semi-additive measures on an inventory snapshot: not a sum, but something like the period-end value, or an average across the period.
    • Checkpoint

    You should have a working PivotTable, one confirmed-sensible additive summary (by warehouse or by product, at a single point in time), and one written sentence explaining why summing across dates for a single warehouse/product is invalid — plus what the correct alternative aggregation would be.
