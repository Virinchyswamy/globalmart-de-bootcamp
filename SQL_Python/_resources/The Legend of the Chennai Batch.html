Chapter One

The Great Arrival (Or: "Sir, Is the WiFi Working?")

8:47 AM — Tredence Chennai, 3rd Floor
1

The Tredence Chennai office smelled of fresh filter coffee and quiet ambition on a perfectly ordinary morning — until 25 data engineering learners arrived, and it stopped being ordinary forever.

T Nikshitha was, as always, the first one in. She had arrived at 8:43 AM, set up her laptop, colour-coded her notebook tabs (pink for SQL, green for Python, blue for "things Virinchy Sir said that I need to Google later"), and was already scanning the room like a hawk waiting for lunch time — which, at 8:44 AM, was still four hours and sixteen minutes away. She knew. She was counting.

At 8:52 AM, Yogesh Patidar burst through the door like he had somewhere more important to be than the place he was already arriving at. He dropped into a chair, opened his laptop, and started answering questions that hadn't been asked yet.

The two Amans arrived together — Aman Shankar Verma and Aman Thapliyal — which would cause Virinchy Sir exactly eleven moments of confusion throughout the day. They walked in mid-debate.

Aman S.
I'm telling you, a NULL is basically the universe saying "I don't know yet."
Aman T.
It is NOT the universe. It's just a missing value in a database.
Aman S.
But what IS a missing value, philosophically?

Amardeep Meena walked in texting his mother: "Reached office safely; training starting soon;" — with a semicolon. This was not the last semicolon Amardeep would type today. Not even close.

Harsh Kumar entered loudly, laughing at something on his phone. He sat down still laughing, opened his laptop, typed one line of SQL, got an error, and laughed louder. Hritin Raj slid in quietly and opened no fewer than six Stack Overflow tabs before anyone said good morning.

Kumar Pratik connected to the WiFi, ran a speed test, and had already read ahead three modules by the time Virinchy Sir opened his slide deck.

Sujit Kumar greeted everyone cheerfully, sat down, and immediately raised his hand.

Sujit Kumar
Sir, before we start — which company pays the most for someone who knows Databricks?
Virinchy Sir
…We haven't started yet, Sujit.
Sujit Kumar
I know sir. I'm planning ahead.

Shivam Kumar was trying to install Python on his phone. Devanand P was writing a comment block above a SELECT * that explained, in painstaking detail, what SELECT * does. Prishita Shukla had already uncapped three highlighters. S R Sneha was Googling "is Python case sensitive" for what she would later confirm was not her first time.

And then, from the back, Heemansh Bhawsar — who had been so quiet that three people hadn't noticed he was there — said nothing at all. He just smiled and opened his terminal. He would later ask the most thoughtful question of the entire day, but that was hours away.

At exactly 9:01 AM, Virinchy Sir from Enqurious clicked his first slide.

"Good morning, everyone. Today we begin with SQL. By the end of this week, you'll be thinking in queries."

— Virinchy Sir, 9:01 AM, unaware of what he was unleashing
Chapter Two

SELECT * FROM brain WHERE coffee > 0

9:15 AM — First Query Exercise
2

The first exercise was simple: write a query to join the transactions and products tables on product_id. Virinchy Sir had barely finished the sentence when Yogesh Patidar had already typed his answer, hit run, and was looking around waiting for the next question.

Kumar Pratik finished two seconds later. He then opened the next module and started reading about window functions, which they weren't covering until Day 5.

Bysani Karthik stared at the blank query editor for thirty seconds, then looked up.

Bysani Karthik
Sir, can we just do this in Excel? I have VLOOKUP mapped to a shortcut key.
Virinchy Sir
Karthik, the dataset has 4.7 million rows.
Bysani Karthik
...I have a fast laptop.

Meanwhile, Devanand P had written his query. All forty-seven lines of it. He had added a comment above every clause.

-- This SELECT statement selects the columns we want to select SELECT -- t refers to the transactions table (aliased as t above) t.order_id, -- p refers to the products table (aliased as p below) p.product_name, -- Sales_Amount is the amount of the sale in sales currency t.Sales_Amount -- ... (continues for 41 more lines) ...

"Devanand," said Virinchy Sir gently, "your query is correct. But it's also the length of a short story."

"Thank you sir," said Devanand, missing the point entirely. "I can add more comments if needed."

Over at her desk, Payal had been quietly working through the exercise when she noticed something. She squinted at the slide. She looked again. She raised her hand.

Payal
Sir, I think there's a typo in Slide 12. The column name in the code is product_Id but the table has product_id — lowercase d.

Virinchy Sir looked at the slide. He looked at the table. He looked back at the slide. Thirty seconds passed.

Virinchy Sir
...Yes. That's a typo. Good catch, Payal. Everyone else, please update your notes.
Prishita Shukla
Already done. I used red pen for errors.

Raj Sharma had been staring at the JOIN syntax with increasing suspicion.

Raj Sharma
Sir, I just want to understand — why can't I use VLOOKUP?
Bysani Karthik
Same question sir.
Virinchy Sir
Gentlemen. There is no VLOOKUP in SQL. There is no spreadsheet. There are only queries.
Raj Sharma
What about INDEX MATCH?

From the back of the room, Heemansh Bhawsar said nothing. He had already finished the exercise, run it successfully, and was now reading the pandas documentation.

At 9:47 AM, Amardeep Meena ran his first successful query and instinctively typed a semicolon in the Slack channel to announce it. Saket Ranjan, noticing the LEFT JOIN in the exercise, raised his hand.

Saket Ranjan
Sir, LEFT JOIN — does this mean only the left side of the body? Like, for the LEFT specifically?
Virinchy Sir
Saket. It refers to the left table in the query. Not the left side of anything anatomical.
Saket Ranjan
Okay sir. And RIGHT JOIN is the other table?
Virinchy Sir
Yes. Exactly.
Saket Ranjan
And FULL OUTER JOIN is for when you skip leg day?

Harsh Kumar laughed so loudly that someone on the second floor looked up from their laptop.

Chapter Three

The Python Pandemonium

10:30 AM — Python & Pandas
3

The shift to Python produced a new and interesting chaos. Virinchy Sir introduced the pandas library. P V Ganadeep wrote it down carefully in his notes as PAND-us. He would spend three days pronouncing it this way with complete confidence.

P V Ganadeep
Sir, this PAND-us library — it is only for data analysis, or also for machine learning?
Virinchy Sir
It's — Ganadeep, just to confirm, you mean… pandas? The library?
P V Ganadeep
Yes sir, PAND-us. I just said.

Shivam Kumar had, by now, accepted that Python was not going to run on his phone and had switched to his laptop. He pip install-ed eleven packages, several of which were not relevant to anything being taught. He then got a dependency conflict error and stared at it with the philosophical stillness of someone watching rain.

Sushant Kumar had loaded his DataFrame. He then ran df.head(). He looked at the output. He ran df.head() again. And again. On the sixth run, M Gaurav Kumar, who sat next to him, leaned over.

M Gaurav Kumar
Bhai, the data is not going to change.
Sushant Kumar
I know. I just like to be sure.

He ran it four more times before proceeding.

Hritin Raj had found a Stack Overflow answer from 2019 that seemed to solve the exercise. He copy-pasted it. It worked perfectly. He sat back with a troubled expression.

Hritin Raj
Sir, it worked. But I don't know why.
Virinchy Sir
That is both the beauty and the danger of Stack Overflow, Hritin.

M Gaurav Kumar had, at this point, asked eleven questions in forty-five minutes. They were all good questions. Virinchy Sir was impressed but also slightly tired. S R Sneha was Googling "pandas case sensitive column names" and then, a few minutes later, "is Python case sensitive" — again. Sukrit Ghosh had forty-seven browser tabs open. When someone pointed this out, he said, "I know which one I need," and opened a new one.

Then Remalli Kamal Kumar, who had been working quietly and intensely, accidentally typed something in his terminal. The room went silent because everyone could see his screen.

$ git push --force origin main
Remalli Kamal Kumar
Oh no. I was in the wrong terminal window.
Prishita Shukla
That's a shared repo isn't it?
Virinchy Sir
Everyone close your terminals. Kamal — put your hands up where I can see them.

It was a personal repo. No data was harmed. Harsh Kumar laughed for four minutes.

"Sir, in Rajasthan, we have a system for handling this kind of situation too."

— Santosh Kumar, to nobody's complete surprise

Nobody asked what the Rajasthan system was. Santosh Kumar explained it anyway. It was, by all accounts, a completely different system that somehow arrived at the same answer.

Meanwhile, Aviskar M Dhavale had been following along carefully — until Virinchy Sir reached the most critical part of the np.where() explanation, at which exact moment Aviskar's laptop screen went dark. He had accidentally pressed the power button instead of the Escape key. By the time it came back on, the slide had changed.

Aviskar M Dhavale
Sir, can you go back? I think I missed something important.
Virinchy Sir
Aviskar, that was the single most important slide of the session.
Aviskar M Dhavale
I know sir. The timing was very unfortunate.

Chapter Five

pivot_table Strikes Back

2:15 PM — Post-Lunch Afternoon
5

The post-lunch session was known, in most training programmes, as the Valley of Shadows — that gentle 2 PM haze where the sambar weighs heavily on the mind and the screen starts to blur. Virinchy Sir, who had seen many batches, was prepared.

He was not fully prepared for Heemansh Bhawsar.

Heemansh, who had been the quietest presence in the room all day, raised his hand at 2:17 PM and asked a question so precise and so unexpectedly deep about DataFrame indexing that Virinchy Sir stopped, looked at his slides, looked at Heemansh, and said, "That is — actually an extremely good question. Where did that come from?"

Heemansh shrugged. "I was thinking at lunch."

The pivot table exercise arrived like a plot twist. The task was clear: take the long-format monthly sales data and pivot it so months became columns. Fourteen people immediately understood. Nine people ran .melt() instead. Two people accidentally dropped the DataFrame entirely. Sushant Kumar ran df.head() six times before attempting it.

Aman Thapliyal
Sir, I did the pivot. But the months are in the wrong order — January comes after April.
Virinchy Sir
That's because they're sorted alphabetically as strings. You need to sort the columns after pivoting.
Aman Thapliyal
Alphabetically? April before January is correct alphabetically though, sir.
Virinchy Sir
Yes. That's the problem.

Raj Sharma had pivoted successfully but was visibly unsatisfied.

Raj Sharma
Sir, this is basically a pivot table. Like in Excel.
Virinchy Sir
Yes, Raj. It is exactly like a pivot table. That's why it's called pivot_table.
Raj Sharma
So I could have used Excel.
Virinchy Sir
Raj. The dataset. Four point seven million rows.
Raj Sharma
Right. Right. No. I know.

Prishita Shukla had added a fourth highlighter colour: orange, for "things Raj says that technically aren't wrong."

At 3:45 PM, Aman Shankar Verma raised his hand for the first time since morning.

Aman Shankar Verma
Sir, when we do .melt() to go from wide to long — is that like… unbecoming? Like the data is shedding its shape?
Virinchy Sir
…In a sense, yes. That is actually a reasonable way to think about it.
Aman Shankar Verma
Data has an ego, sir. We must respect its transformations.

P V Ganadeep nodded slowly. Harsh Kumar was already laughing.

By 4:30 PM, Kumar Pratik had finished all exercises including the bonus ones, reviewed the next day's material, built a small helper function to automate a repetitive step, and was now reading a Medium article about Spark optimisation. Yogesh Patidar, not to be outdone, had answered three of Virinchy Sir's rhetorical questions. Correctly.

S R Sneha, at 4:48 PM, Googled "is pandas case sensitive." This was a different question from "is Python case sensitive," and showed meaningful growth.

Chapter Six

The Grand DENSE_RANK() of the Chennai Batch

5:30 PM — End of Day Ceremony
6

As the day wound down, Virinchy Sir looked at his batch — tired, caffeinated, slightly confused, but unmistakably sharper than they had been eight hours ago. He decided that if DENSE_RANK() could rank vendors, it could certainly rank this batch. Not by performance — but by the one quality each of them had shown in abundance.

DENSE_RANK() OVER (ORDER BY Uniqueness DESC)

6:00 PM — The Hero's Journey Begins

They came in the morning as twenty-five people who had heard of SQL. They left in the evening as twenty-five people who had written it, broken it, fixed it, argued about it, laughed at it, and — somewhere between the LEFT JOINs and the pivot tables and the semicolons and the forty-seven browser tabs — begun to understand it.

Virinchy Sir packed his laptop. He looked at the room — the empty chairs, the half-finished coffee cups, the whiteboard still covered in aggregate functions. He smiled.

Tomorrow, they would learn window functions. Yogesh had already read ahead. Pratik had finished all the exercises. Nikshitha had already set her alarm.

Bysani sent a message in the group chat at 6:04 PM.

Bysani Karthik
Good session today. Sir, one question — can DENSE_RANK do this in Excel?

Amardeep replied: No Karthik;
