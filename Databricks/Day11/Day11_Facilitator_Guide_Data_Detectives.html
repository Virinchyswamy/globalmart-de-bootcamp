The Answer Key

All 6 Real Causes, With Evaluation Rubrics

Use the Strong/Shallow columns to evaluate report-outs live, and to know what to push on if a group's theory is close but incomplete.

A

Major Eating Federation

Real Cause

Two contestants with near-identical names ("R. Deshmukh" and "R. Deshmukhh") got conflated in the roster. The "impossible" total isn't one person's real number — it's the combined total of two different athletes' results, merged under one row because the near-duplicate name wasn't caught. This is a real, common data-quality failure mode: entity resolution — matching records by name instead of a stable unique ID.

Strong
  • Notices the impossible total ≈ sum of two real entries
  • Catches the near-duplicate name spelling
  • Names "no stable unique ID" as the structural problem, not just "a typo"
Shallow
  • "Maybe he trained really hard" / assumes it's a real feat
  • Says "data entry error" without identifying which entry or why
  • Never engages with the near-duplicate-name clue at all
B

Golden Comb Apiary Co-op

Real Cause

Colony Collapse Disorder (CCD) — a real, documented phenomenon (widely reported since ~2006) where worker bees abandon a hive en masse, leaving the queen and brood behind, with no single agreed cause. Research generally implicates a combination of Varroa mites, pesticide exposure (especially neonicotinoids), disease, and colony stress — not one clean answer.

Strong
  • Names CCD specifically
  • Acknowledges multiple contributing factors, doesn't force one cause
  • Connects "queen/brood present, workers gone" directly to CCD's defining symptom
Shallow
  • Picks one cause (e.g. "pesticides") as definitively settled
  • Doesn't explain why workers specifically vanished vs. the whole hive dying
  • Treats it as regional weather/disease without naming the actual phenomenon
C

Long Wing Racing Pigeon Club

Real Cause

A geomagnetic storm / solar flare disrupted the pigeons' magnetic-field-based navigation that day. This is a real, studied phenomenon — racing pigeon federations and researchers have documented mass "smashes" (bad race days with heavy losses) correlated with solar/geomagnetic activity, even in otherwise clear weather.

Strong
  • Identifies this as environmental/atmospheric, not local weather
  • Names solar or geomagnetic disruption specifically
  • Explains why it hit every loft simultaneously despite clear skies
Shallow
  • Blames generic "bad luck" or predators
  • Doesn't explain why it was room-wide/every-loft, not one route
  • Ignores the "weather was fine" clue entirely
D

Locked In Escape Experiences

Real Cause

Daylight Saving Time's "spring forward" clock jump broke naive timestamp-difference math. On the day clocks skip forward an hour, a session that starts at 1:45 AM and looks like it "ends" at 1:20 AM (because 2:00-3:00 AM never happened on the clock) produces a negative or nonsensical end_time - start_time. It recurs every year because DST happens every year, on a similar calendar date.

Strong
  • Names DST explicitly
  • Explains clocks "skip" an hour, breaking naive subtraction
  • Connects this directly to why it recurs annually on a similar date
Shallow
  • "Maybe staff made the puzzles easier that week"
  • Doesn't address the negative-duration clue at all
  • Doesn't explain the yearly recurrence
E

Great Glen Highland Games Association

Real Cause

Caber toss is scored on how close to a straight 12-o'clock rotation/landing angle the caber flips — not distance thrown. A shorter, perfectly-turned toss outscores a longer one that lands crooked or doesn't fully flip. The data team assumed "bigger number wins," like every other throwing event, and built the ranking with the wrong column's semantics — this isn't a data error at all, it's a domain-knowledge/business-logic error.

Strong
  • Correctly states caber toss's real scoring rule (rotation/angle, not distance)
  • Recognizes this as a wrong assumption, not corrupted data
  • Explains why the same logic works fine for hammer/weight throw but not this event
Shallow
  • Assumes the distance numbers themselves got swapped/corrupted
  • Doesn't identify the actual real scoring rule
  • Treats it as a one-off data entry mistake rather than a systemic assumption
F

World Air Guitar Championship

Real Cause

A non-competitive "Warm-Up" exhibition segment's audience-hype scores got recorded in the same "score" column as the real judged competitive rounds, since the spreadsheet has no field distinguishing round type. Averaging that column blends two incompatible kinds of scores. (World Air Guitar Championship is a real event, Oulu, Finland, since 1996 — with a real official judging term, "airness.")

Strong
  • Spots the "Warm-Up" label in the evidence table as the smoking gun
  • Names this a schema/source-mixing problem, not a judging problem
  • Proposes a fix (a round-type or is_competitive flag)
Shallow
  • Assumes one judge was biased or made an error
  • Doesn't notice the "Warm-Up" round label at all
  • Doesn't connect the single unlabeled "score" column to the mixing problem
If Time Allows / For the Reveal

Debrief Question Bank

Built around the fact that each case landed on 3-4 different people who researched it completely independently.

  • For any case where multiple reps presented conflicting theories: "You both researched this independently — what specifically made you land on different explanations? Which clue tipped it for you?"
  • Case A specifically: "What would you actually need to check in a real database to confirm two rows got merged, rather than just suspecting it?"
  • Case D specifically: ask both learners who found DST and learners who didn't to defend their theory against "why does this happen at roughly the same time every single year?" — the yearly-recurrence clue is the one a shallow theory always skips.
  • Case E specifically: "If this isn't a data quality bug at all, whose job was it to catch this before the ranking query got built?" — a good prompt for "talk to domain experts before assuming standard semantics."
  • General, ask the whole room: "Which of your case's clues did your own theory explain worst? Would you have caught that gap without this question?"
  • General closing: "Every single one of today's 'broken data' stories turned out to have a completely ordinary, findable explanation once you understood the domain. How often do you think that's true at a real job, versus data actually just being wrong?"

    "Day 5 taught you a rule: don't assume a strange-looking row is broken data before you've actually investigated — some patterns that look wrong are valid business rules, and some that look clean are silently broken.
