Data Detectives: The Case of the Impossible Numbers

Every dataset below comes from a real, legitimate industry — just one you've probably never worked in. Something in each one has gone strange: a number that shouldn't be possible, a pattern that makes no sense, a leaderboard that seems backwards. You don't get told why. Your job is to research the industry itself — properly, for real, online — until you know enough about how it actually works to explain what most likely happened, and defend that theory out loud.

🕵️ 6 cases, cycled 1-per-person around the room 🔬 Minimum 30 min of real research, no shortcuts ⏱ 90 minutes total, ends with a live reveal

Before You Do Anything Else

How This Works

Read this whole section before you open a browser tab.

This is individual, same as Day 8. Your table is a seat, not a team. The facilitator will count off 1 through 6 around the whole room, wrapping as needed — that number is your case. The same case will land on 3-4 other people scattered around the room, not necessarily at your table. That's intentional: later, you'll compare notes with exactly those people, and probably not agree.

1 · Research (30 min)

Read your case file below. Then go actually learn about the real industry it's set in — not the anomaly, the industry. How it's really scored, run, regulated, measured. The anomaly will start making sense once you understand the domain, not before.

2 · Write Your Theory (15 min)

Connect what you researched to the clues in your case file. Write your best explanation for what actually happened — in plain language, like you're explaining it to someone who wasn't there.

3 · Compare & Reveal (35 min)

Find the others who got your same case number, compare theories for a few minutes, then one of you presents your group's best theory to the whole room. Facilitator reveals what actually happened after all 6 have gone.

Why no answer key up front. On the job, nobody will hand you an explanation for a weird number either — you'll have to go figure out enough about the business and the domain to make a case for what happened, then defend it when someone pushes back. That's the actual skill this hour is practicing. The reveal at the end isn't a test you pass or fail — it's there so you find out how close real research got you, and where the gap was.

Phase 1 ⏱ 30 min minimum

How to Actually Research This

"Look it up" isn't a plan. Work through these, in order, for whichever industry you were assigned:

  • Learn how the industry actually measures/scores/records the thing your anomaly is about. Is there an official body, a standard rulebook, a sanctioning organization? Most of these industries have one — find it.
  • Learn what's considered normal. What's a typical, realistic value for the number in your case file? Knowing what's normal is what lets you spot exactly how the anomaly deviates from it.
  • Look for a real event, rule, or quirk specific to this industry that could plausibly explain the pattern. This is the actual research — not guessing, finding something real that fits.
  • Re-read your clues one more time once you know the domain. At least one clue will suddenly make a lot more sense than it did on first read.

    • A good theory names a specific, real, checkable cause — not "the data must be wrong somehow." "The data must be wrong somehow" is true of every mystery ever and explains nothing. Your job is to say how and why, in a way someone could actually go verify.

    The Major Eating Federation — Competitive Eating

    Case Assigned To: 4 Detectives Around the Room

    Payal was updating the official leaderboard for last month's Founder's Day Classic when one row made her stop scrolling. A contestant's recorded total for the 10-minute event was nearly double the current world record — for anything, in any eating discipline, ever recorded by the Federation. Everyone remembers who actually won that day. It wasn't this number.

    Clues

    • The impossible total appears exactly once, for exactly one event, for exactly one contestant.
    • That same contestant's totals at every other event that season are completely normal.
    • Two names on the Federation's full roster differ by a single character — a repeated letter, an extra space, nothing a human would notice scanning a list.
    • The impossible total is suspiciously close to the sum of two other real contestants' totals from that same event.

    B

    Golden Comb Apiary Co-operative — Beekeeping

    Case Assigned To: 4 Detectives Around the Room

    Aman was double-checking the co-op's quarterly harvest numbers when the Northfield region's report stopped him cold. Every single hive in Northfield produced roughly 90% less honey than last year. Fifteen kilometers away, in Eastbrook — same climate, same season, same co-op — yields look completely normal. Nobody sprayed anything unusual in Northfield. Nobody changed how the hives were managed. The bees just... didn't come back with much.

    Clues

    • The drop hit every hive in the region, not just one or two — and it happened in one season, not gradually over years.
    • Worker bee population counts in the hive logs dropped sharply mid-season, not slowly.
    • The co-op's own pesticide-application records show nothing unusual logged for Northfield that season.
    • Beekeepers reported finding hives with the queen and some brood still present, but far fewer worker bees than expected — almost like the workers simply never returned.

    • What large-scale phenomena have historically caused sudden regional honeybee population crashes?
    • Is there a name for a situation where worker bees leave and simply don't return, but the queen/brood remain?

    C

    Long Wing Racing Pigeon Club

    Case Assigned To: 4 Detectives Around the Room

    Hritin was logging results for the club's biggest race of the year — 400km, dozens of lofts entered — when he noticed almost every single bird came home late. Not one unlucky loft. Nearly all of them, on the same day, by roughly the same margin. A few dozen birds across different lofts never came home at all. The weather that day was clear. Visibility was fine. Nothing about the race conditions looked unusual on paper.

    Clues

    • Average return time for this one race is more than double the club's five-year average.
    • The pattern hit every loft entered, not a specific handler or a specific route.
    • Weather logs for race day show clear skies, mild wind, good visibility — nothing that normally slows birds down.
    • The "lost bird" count (never returned at all) for this single race is unusually high compared to every other race that season.

    • How do racing pigeons actually find their way home over long distances?
    • What natural, non-weather phenomena are documented to interfere with long-distance animal navigation?
    • Is there anything datable/newsworthy that tends to disrupt navigation systems more broadly on a specific day?

    D

    Locked In Escape Experiences

    Case Assigned To: 4 Detectives Around the Room

    Harsh was reviewing solve-time analytics for the flagship "Vault of Secrets" room when the weekly chart looked flat-out broken. For exactly one week, average solve times crash to almost nothing — a few entries in the raw log even show a negative duration, which obviously can't happen in real life. No puzzles changed that week. Staff logs show totally normal group sizes and totally normal-looking gameplay. And strangely, the same broken week shows up again the following year, right around the same dates.

    Clues

    • The anomaly is confined to one specific week each year, not random.
    • A handful of raw log rows show end_time − start_time as a negative number.
    • No puzzle, staffing, or pricing changes were logged during the affected week.
    • Groups who played that week reported totally normal experiences when asked — nothing felt different to them.
    DateGroupStart TimeEnd TimeRecorded Duration
    Mar 08G-11802:1502:52-8 min
    Mar 08G-11903:0003:41-19 min
    Mar 15G-14102:1002:5545 min
    Research this
    • What happens to clocks in many countries on specific dates, twice a year? What date does one of those changes fall on?
    • If a system just subtracts one clock-reading timestamp from another, what could go wrong on exactly that kind of date?
    • Why would this same problem recur on a similar date every single year, automatically?

    E

    Great Glen Highland Games Association — Caber Toss

    Case Assigned To: 4 Detectives Around the Room

    Santosh was building the season leaderboard when the rankings came out backwards from what everyone at the games actually remembered. The athlete with the shortest recorded caber toss of the year is ranked champion. The athlete whose toss went the farthest by a wide margin is sitting near the bottom of the table. Every other throwing event at the same games — hammer throw, weight throw — ranks in exactly the order everyone expects. Just this one event looks wrong.

    Clues

    • Only the caber toss leaderboard is scrambled — every other event ranks normally.
    • The ranking logic used for every event, including caber toss, is identical: sort by recorded number, biggest wins.
    • The athlete ranked champion has, by far, the shortest recorded distance in the caber toss table.
    • Officials at the actual event insist the champion's toss was, by their own judging, clearly the best of the day.
    AthleteEventRecorded Distance (m)Season Rank
    D. FraserCaber Toss3.11
    M. AinsleyCaber Toss6.87
    D. FraserHammer Throw28.42
    Research this
    • How is a caber toss actually judged in real Highland Games competition?
    • Is "distance thrown" even the primary thing being scored? What does a genuinely perfect toss require?
    • Why would "bigger distance = better," which works for hammer throw, be the wrong assumption specifically for this event?

    F

    World Air Guitar Championship — National Qualifier

    Case Assigned To: 3 Detectives Around the Room

    Bysani was compiling the national qualifier's final scores when one round's numbers looked completely out of place — nearly double the average of every other round that night, across every judge, for every contestant who played it. Nobody who was actually in the room that evening remembers that round being unusually spectacular. If anything, people remember it as the loosest, most relaxed part of the night.

    Clues

    • Exactly one round's scores are far higher than every other round's, across every judge.
    • The unusually high scores all come from one contiguous block of the evening's judging sheets.
    • The night's official program included a "just for fun," not-officially-judged segment scheduled right before the real competitive rounds began.
    • The scoring spreadsheet has exactly one column labeled "score" — nothing distinguishes what kind of round it was.
    RoundContestantJudgeScore
    Round 1P. NairJudge A7.2
    Warm-UpAudience VolunteerJudge A9.8
    Round 2P. NairJudge B7.5
    Research this
    • What are the real, official judging criteria for competitive air guitar? (Yes — it's a real, official sport, with an official-sounding scoring term.)
    • Do events like this typically run a non-competitive/exhibition segment alongside the real competition on the same night?
    • What tends to happen when two very differently-scored kinds of rounds end up in the same spreadsheet column?

    What a Strong Theory Covers

    This is the same discipline Day 5 asked of you. Back then, the rule was: don't assume a strange-looking row is broken data before you've actually investigated it — some patterns that look wrong are valid, and some that look clean are silently broken. Today's version of that same rule, just dressed up as a pigeon-racing mystery instead of a DQ scan.
