Where We Are

ILT 1 settled what "a sale" means for GlobalMart and picked the star schema shape. ILT 2 locked the grain — one row per order line item — and designed all 6 dimensions: dim_customer, dim_product, dim_date, dim_address, dim_payment_method, dim_orders.

Two questions are still open, and both live inside the fact table itself: what type of fact table is fact_sales, and what kind of measures does it hold? That's today.

fact_sales's 4 Real Measures

Every number column in fact_sales is tempting to just SUM() when you want a total. But summing the wrong column silently produces a number that looks correct and isn't — nobody gets an error, they just get a wrong dashboard. So before we touch any of them, we need to know which ones are actually safe to add up.

fact_sales has exactly 4 measures. Only 2 of them are safe to SUM().

Safe to sum (additive): Quantity_purchased, Sales_amount.
Not safe to sum (non-additive): Actual_price, Discounted_price.

Fact Table Types

Three Types of Fact Tables

The difference is about when a row is written and whether it ever changes afterward.

Why Bother Classifying a Fact Table At All?

Before you write a single line of pipeline code for a fact table, you have to answer a very practical question: once a row lands in this table, will it ever need to change later — or is it done the moment it's written?

Get that wrong and you build the wrong pipeline. Assume a row never changes when it actually does, and your table quietly goes stale — nobody edits it, so it stops matching reality. Assume a row might change when it actually never does, and you've built expensive update logic (checking "did this row change?" on every run) for a table that was always going to be insert-only.

That one question — "does a row get written once and left alone, or does it get revisited and updated?" — is exactly what sorts every fact table into one of three buckets. The formal names for those buckets are transactional, periodic snapshot, and accumulating snapshot.

The Three Types

Transactional Fact — fact_sales is this type

Something happens at one exact moment — a purchase, a withdrawal — and you write one row to record it. That row is the permanent record of that single event; there's nothing left to "update" once it's written, because the event itself is already over.

GlobalMart: one row per order line item, inserted the moment it's placed. Append-only — same discipline you already know from Bronze/Silver.

Periodic Snapshot Fact — not used today

Some things don't have a single "it happened" moment — they're a state that exists continuously (like how much stock is sitting in a warehouse). To track that, you take a measurement on a fixed schedule — every day, every week — and write a row each time, whether or not anything actually changed.

A hypothetical GlobalMart example (not built here): fact_inventory_daily — one row per product per day.

Accumulating Snapshot Fact — not used today

Some events aren't instant — they're a process with stages spread over time (placed, then shipped, then delivered, days apart). Instead of writing a new row at every stage, you write ONE row for the whole process and keep updating it as each stage happens. This is the one type that is not append-only.

Hypothetical: an order-lifecycle fact — order_placed_date → shipped_date → actual_delivery_date, same row, updated again and again as the order moves.

Apply the test from the previous slide to fact_sales: does a row ever get revisited and updated after it's written, or is it done the moment it lands? A row in fact_sales represents one order line item being purchased — a single moment, not a recurring measurement and not a multi-stage process. So it's written once, when Bronze/Silver capture that line item, and Gold never comes back to edit Quantity_purchased or Discounted_price afterward. That makes fact_sales a transactional fact table.

Measures

Additive, Semi-Additive, Non-Additive

fact_sales carries exactly 4 measures. S R Sneha's rule of thumb, before she ever types SUM(): if I add these two numbers together, would a business person actually recognize the result as meaningful? That one question sorts every measure into one of three buckets.

fact_sales's 4 Measures

Additive — Quantity_purchased · Sales_amount

These two answer "how much in total?" — and a total is exactly what adding numbers together is for. Add up Sales_amount from a thousand different line items, across any mix of products, customers, or dates, and the result is still a real number a business person recognizes: total revenue. That's what makes something safe to SUM() — the sum itself still means something.

Sneha needs this month's revenue by category for a stakeholder deck. She just runs SUM(Sales_amount) — by category, by customer, by year, it's correct every time. This is "revenue."

Non-Additive — Actual_price · Discounted_price

Say one line item sold for ₹500 and another, totally different product, sold for ₹1,500. Add those two prices together and you get ₹2,000 — but ₹2,000 of what? It isn't total revenue (revenue also depends on how many units of each were bought — that's what Sales_amount is for). It isn't an average price either (a plain sum isn't divided by anything). It's just two unrelated per-unit prices stuck together, and it doesn't answer any real business question. That's why Actual_price and Discounted_price are never safe to SUM().

Wrong: AVG(Discounted_price) across many products — same problem, just divided. Right: average selling price = SUM(Sales_amount)/SUM(Quantity_purchased), because that actually weights the average by how many units sold at each price.

Semi-Additive — not in fact_sales

Some measures are safe to add across most things, but break specifically when you add across time — because they're a snapshot of a level, not a count of something that happened. Add today's stock across 3 warehouses (100 + 100 + 100 = 300 units) and that's real — 300 units exist right now. But add one warehouse's 100 units across 7 different days (100 × 7 = 700) and that number is meaningless — it's the same 100 units, sitting there, counted seven separate times. Nothing actually adds up to 700. That's what "semi-additive" means: sum across space, never across time.

Sneha keeps expecting fact_sales to hand her a semi-additive measure to be careful with. It never does — this bucket exists so she recognizes one the day she meets it elsewhere, like inventory-on-hand or an account balance.

So is AVG() ever allowed on these two columns? Once, and it's a special case. Day 7 HOL 2's real vw_monthly_category_sales view runs ROUND(AVG(Actual_price - Discounted_price), 2) AS avg_discount_given. Notice what's actually being averaged: not the raw price of a product, but the gap between two prices — how big a discount was given. That gap is a rate, not a raw price, and averaging a rate across many line items to see "how big a discount do we typically give" is a meaningful business question. Averaging raw prices across unrelated products still isn't — the rule doesn't change, this just isn't that case.

GlobalMart's Twist on the Textbook Case

Degenerate Dimensions

Sometimes an ID sits inside a fact table with nothing else describing it — no separate table needed, because there's genuinely nothing more to say about it than the ID itself. That kind of ID has a name: a degenerate dimension. Sneha is about to run into a GlobalMart case that bends this definition in a way worth slowing down for.

Order_ID Looks Like a Classic Candidate

By that definition...

Sneha looks at Order_ID sitting inside fact_sales. It's just an ID column — no channel, no shipping tier, nothing else describing it sits next to it in the fact table. That matches the definition exactly, so she calls it correctly: "classic degenerate-dimension candidate — just an ID, nothing more." Textbook-perfect instinct.

Then she notices something that seems to contradict her own call: GlobalMart built dim_orders anyway — this afternoon's Hands-On constructs it from <your-catalog>.silver.orders, complete with its own surrogate key (order_sk) and real describing columns: order_channel, shipping_tier_id, supplier_id, order_date. If Order_ID truly had "nothing more to say about it," why would a whole dimension table exist for it?

So which is it — degenerate, or a real dimension? Both. Sneha didn't get it wrong, and dim_orders doesn't prove her wrong either — the next slide walks through exactly why both are true at once.

Why Both Can Be True

This isn't a contradiction — it's two different tables asking two different questions of the same ID, and getting two different honest answers.

Question 1 — what does dim_orders need Order_ID for? Its whole job is to describe an order: what channel it came through, what shipping tier, which supplier. Those are real, useful things to know about an order on their own — a business user can ask "how many orders came through the app channel this month?" without ever touching fact_sales. That's a real, standalone dimension, full stop.

Question 2 — what does fact_sales need Order_ID for? Nothing that detailed. At the line-item level, fact_sales never needs to know the channel or the shipping tier — it just needs a way to group line items back into the order they belong to, and to count "how many separate orders" versus "how many line items." That's it. And doing exactly that — sitting in the fact table as a plain ID, with no join needed to do its job — is precisely what a degenerate dimension is for.

The resolution: "is Order_ID degenerate?" was never a property of the ID by itself — it's a property of what a particular table needs from it. dim_orders needs description, so from its own point of view it's a real dimension. fact_sales only needs grouping and counting, so from its point of view Order_ID still behaves exactly like a degenerate dimension. Same column, two tables, two honest answers — at the same time.

Looking From Which Table?

Laid out side by side — same column, two honest answers depending which table is asking:

From fact_sales's Point of View, Order_ID Still Does the Degenerate-Dimension Job

Even without ever joining order_sk, Order_ID still earns its keep inside fact_sales — it does three real jobs:

  1. COUNT(DISTINCT Order_ID) answers "how many orders?" — different from "how many line items?" (Day 7 HOL 2's real vw_regional_sales view uses exactly this)
  2. It groups line items back into their parent order
  3. It supports drill-through back to the source order record

The Actual Lesson

The takeaway to carry forward: building a real, standalone dimension table doesn't force every fact table that touches it to join through its surrogate key. fact_sales still connects to dim_orders using Order_ID as a natural key — it just never goes through order_sk to get there.

And this isn't special treatment just for orders — the exact same natural-key simplification applies to all six of fact_sales's dimensions, which is the next section.

One heads-up before we get there: Payment_ID is not just "the Order_ID story, repeated." It's a stricter case — full explanation, with a concrete example, coming up shortly.

Key Design

Why fact_sales Uses Natural Keys

Every one of fact_sales's 6 dimensions already has a perfectly good surrogate key sitting there, ready to join on. fact_sales uses almost none of them. This section is about why — and what it costs.

Every Dimension Already Has a Proper Surrogate Key

customer_sk / product_sk (carried over from Silver's SCD2 build), address_sk / payment_method_sk / order_sk (generated fresh in Gold), and dim_date.date_key (the one exception, shaped as YYYYMMDD).

Why does the standard rule insist on joining through the surrogate key instead of the plain business ID? Because for any dimension that keeps history — like dim_customer, which never overwrites an old row when something changes — the same natural ID can end up pointing at more than one row over time. The surrogate key is what pins a fact row down to exactly one specific version of that dimension row: the version that was true at the moment the sale happened. Join on the bare natural ID instead, and there's no way to say which version you meant.

That reasoning is exactly what the Kimball-standard rule — and cert material — is built on: a fact table should join to its dimensions on the surrogate key, and never the natural key.

fact_sales Does Not Follow That Rule

Given everything on the last slide, you'd expect fact_sales to join in using customer_sk, product_sk, order_sk, address_sk, and dim_date.date_key. It doesn't. Instead, it carries the plain business IDs themselves — Customer_ID, Product_ID, Order_ID, Address_ID, Payment_ID, and Time_ID — six natural keys, not six surrogate keys.

Five of them still join their dimension directly, just on the natural key instead of the surrogate one: Customer_ID→dim_customer, Product_ID→dim_product, Order_ID→dim_orders, Address_ID→dim_address, Time_ID→dim_date (Time_ID is, literally, dim_date.date_key).

The One Real Exception, and the One Real Key

Payment_ID is the odd one out — and seeing the actual values makes it obvious why. A Payment_ID value looks like PAY-001 — it identifies one specific payment event, this one exact transaction where this one order got paid for. dim_payment_method's ID looks like PM-002 — it identifies a payment method, a category like "Credit Card" or "UPI," and there are only about 5 of those in the entire business.

Thousands of different Payment_IDs can all share the same method category. Trying to match PAY-001 against PM-002 isn't a missing join waiting to be added — it's comparing two different kinds of thing that were never built to line up: one ID counts individual transactions, the other counts categories. That's why Payment_ID doesn't resolve to dim_payment_method through any column at all — not even the natural key.

The only surrogate key fact_sales actually generates is its own row identity: fact_sales_sk = sha2(order_item_id, 256) — a key that identifies the fact row itself, not a foreign key pointing anywhere.

Why This Is a Real Cost — Losing Point-in-Time Accuracy

Picture a GlobalMart customer whose email address changes on March 15th. Because dim_customer tracks history (SCD Type 2, already live since Day 5), the OLD row isn't overwritten — it stays on file, closed out with an end date. A brand NEW row is added with the new email, and its own new customer_sk. So now there are two rows for the same Customer_ID: one that was true before March 15th, one that's true after.

fact_sales joins to dim_customer using the bare Customer_ID — not customer_sk. But Customer_ID is identical on both of those rows; it can't tell you which version you meant. So when a query joins on it, there's no way to pick "the version that was true back when this particular sale happened" — the join just resolves to whichever row currently holds that ID, which is always the newest one.

Concretely: query a sale from January, and the join will show you March's email attached to it, not January's — the email that was actually true on the day of that sale is gone from the picture. That's what "losing point-in-time accuracy" means: a natural-key join always shows the customer as they are now, never as they were at the moment of the sale.

Why GlobalMart Accepts That Cost Anyway

ReasonExplanation
UniformityAll 6 dimensions get the exact same natural-key treatment — no special-case join logic to remember dimension by dimension, not even for dim_orders, the newest and most obviously "real dimension" table. One rule to remember, applied everywhere, beats six different rules that are each individually "more correct."
Documented, not hiddenDay 7 HOL 2's own Key Takeaways state it directly: "Natural-key joins were a deliberate choice, not a shortcut — you now know exactly what it costs and why it was still the right call for this program."

Quick-Fire Q&A

Measures & Additivity

Click a question to reveal the answer

Kimball describes three fact table grain types — transaction, periodic snapshot, accumulating snapshot. Which is fact_sales?
Transaction fact table — one row per discrete event (an order line item being purchased), inserted once and mostly never revisited. Periodic snapshot would be "inventory level every night"; accumulating snapshot would be "one row per order, updated as it moves ship→deliver→return." fact_sales is neither of those.
The trap question, tied to the whiteboard sketch: my whiteboard labeled fact_sales's measures "semi-additive" — is that right?
Not for the measures this course actually builds. Sales_amount and Quantity_purchased are additive — safely SUM-able across every dimension, including time. Semi-additive means summable across most dimensions but not across time — the classic example is an account balance or inventory-on-hand snapshot, where adding today's balance to yesterday's produces a meaningless number. fact_sales is a transaction-grain table, not a snapshot table, so it has no semi-additive measure — additive and non-additive cover all 4 of its real measures.
Actual_price and Discounted_price are non-additive. What's the right way to compute an average selling price across many order lines, if you can't just AVG() them?
SUM(Sales_amount) / SUM(Quantity_purchased). Averaging a per-unit price directly across products of wildly different prices produces a number no business person would recognize; deriving it from the two additive measures gives the real weighted average.
Is AVG() ever legitimately safe on a non-additive measure?
One documented case: Day 7 HOL 2's real vw_monthly_category_sales view runs AVG(Actual_price - Discounted_price) — that's averaging the difference of two rates (itself a rate, the discount gap), not averaging a raw price across unrelated products. Discount-gap analysis is meaningful; averaging raw prices across different products still isn't.
Quick-Fire Q&A

Degenerate Dimensions & Natural Keys

Click a question to reveal the answer

Sneha says Order_ID is a degenerate dimension. Aman Thapliyal says no, GlobalMart built a full dim_orders with its own surrogate key, so it's a real dimension. Who's right?
Both of them. It depends which table you're standing at. From dim_orders's own point of view — Aman's angle — it's a genuine standalone dimension, independently queryable ("orders by channel") without ever touching fact_sales. From fact_sales's point of view — Sneha's angle — Order_ID still does the degenerate-dimension job: grouping line items back to their parent order, supporting drill-through, answering COUNT(DISTINCT Order_ID). fact_sales just never joins through order_sk to get there.
If dim_orders has a perfectly good surrogate key, why doesn't fact_sales join to it that way?
Because GlobalMart's natural-key simplification applies uniformly to all six dimensions, not selectively — Customer_ID, Product_ID, Order_ID, Address_ID, and Time_ID all skip their surrogate key the same way. It's a documented, deliberate trade-off (point-in-time accuracy lost, in exchange for uniform join logic across every dimension) — not something specific to orders.
Payment_ID also sits in fact_sales as an ID. Is it the same story as Order_ID — skipping a surrogate key but still resolving to its dimension?
No — a stricter case. Order_ID still resolves to dim_orders on the natural key; it just skips the surrogate key. Payment_ID doesn't resolve to dim_payment_method through any column at all — the two live in entirely different ID spaces (payments vs. payment_methods).
Why do measures need to be numeric and additive-by-default — why not just store a descriptive value like "high/medium/low" as a measure?
Because the entire value of a fact table is fast aggregation — SUM/AVG/COUNT across millions of rows — and that only works on numbers. Descriptive categories belong on a dimension (or as an attribute), precisely so they can be used to GROUP BY/filter a measure instead of being summed themselves, which would be meaningless.
