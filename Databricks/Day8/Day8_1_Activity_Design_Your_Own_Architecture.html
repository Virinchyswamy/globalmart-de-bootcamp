Design Your Own Architecture

On Day 1, you sat through GlobalMart's own problem statement and watched an architecture get pitched to you — sources, ingestion, medallion layers, governance, one fact table that mattered more than the rest. Today that flips. Your facilitator will hand you, individually, one of four brand-new problem statements below, from a company you've never worked with, in a domain you haven't touched yet. Your job is to research the real platform services that fit, design your own architecture, and pitch it to the whole room — the same shape of pitch, except this time nobody wrote it for you first, and nobody designs it with you.

Before You Do Anything Else

How This Works

Read this whole section before you open a single browser tab. This activity is individual, start to finish — read the box below carefully before you assume anything about who you're working with today.

This is not a group project. There are 23 of you, seated at 6 tables (five tables of 4, one table of 3). Your table is a seat, not a team. Everyone at your table will be handed a different one of the four problem statements below — cycled A/B/C/D around the table — specifically so that nobody sitting next to you is working on the same scenario. You research alone, design alone, and build your own pitch page alone. You'll present it solo, to the entire class, not just to the people at your table. The same problem statement will come up again at other tables across the room — that's expected, and it's actually useful, because it means later you can compare two independently-built pitches for the same scenario.

The Shape of the ~3 Hours 40 Minutes

  • ~20 min — read your assigned problem statement closely, on your own, and start your research.
  • ~75 min — research the real services that fit each capability your scenario needs, and settle on your own design.
  • ~25 min — copy Day8_Pitch_Template.html and fill it in with your own researched content. This is your individual pitch deliverable.
  • ~92 min — the pitch block: all 23 people present, one after another, to the whole room. Each pitch is 3 minutes to present plus up to 1 minute of facilitator-led Q&A — 4 minutes per person × 23 people = 92 minutes.
  • ~10 min — wrap-up.

Total: ~220 minutes (about 3 hours 40 minutes). Twenty-three individual pitches take real time — this is the honest number, not a rounded-down one.

The Ground Rules

  • 23 learners, 6 tables (5 of 4, 1 of 3) — seating only. The people at your table are not your project team.
  • One problem statement per person. Everyone at your table gets a different one of the four below, cycled A/B/C/D. The same scenario will recur at other tables — that's fine, it's not a duplicate-avoidance thing across the whole room, only within your table.
  • There is no single correct architecture for any of these. Pitches get compared and defended in front of the room — not matched against a hidden answer key.
  • The problem statements below do not name a single Azure or Databricks service, anywhere, on purpose. Figuring out which real service fits which job is the actual assignment, not a detail to skip past.
  • If time is short, the facilitator may compress the pitch block: 2 minutes per person, no live Q&A (one written note from a randomly-picked audience member instead) — that brings the pitch block to ~50 min and the whole activity to about 3 hours.

Why no answer key. Day 1's GlobalMart poster told you what GlobalMart chose and why. That was the right call for Day 1 — you needed a working example before you could build on it for thirteen days. You don't need that anymore. What you need now is practice doing the thing nobody will do for you on the job: reading a business problem, researching what actually exists, and defending a design choice out loud when someone in the room pushes back on it — using your own judgment, not a group's consensus.

Phase 1 ⏱ ~75 min, with design

Go Research First

Before you sketch anything, go find real answers in real documentation — Azure, Databricks, and honestly anywhere else if you genuinely think a different platform fits a capability better. Don't work from memory of what a slide said in Week 1. For each data source in your problem statement, and for the shared platform layer underneath it, go answer these questions for real, on your own:

1Change Data Capture

If a source is a live transactional database that's continuously updated, what's the real way to capture changes out of it near-real-time, without hammering the source system with repeated full queries? What does that actually look like under the hood — polling, log-reading, something else?

2Streaming / High-Frequency Events

If a source fires a high volume of small events continuously — location pings, sensor readings, clickstream — what's built to ingest that volume without falling over or forcing you to batch-poll it? What happens if the pipeline falls behind?

3Batch / File-Based Ingestion

If a source is a file dropped on a schedule (or an irregular schedule) into cloud storage, what's the real mechanism for picking up new files incrementally, without reprocessing everything each time? What happens when the files are messy or inconsistently formatted?

4Storage

Once data lands, where does it actually live, and why is that the right shape for a lakehouse to build Bronze/Silver/Gold on top of, rather than a plain file share or a relational database?

5Transformation / Compute

What actually runs the Bronze → Silver → Gold transformations, on a schedule or continuously, at the volume your scenario implies? What's different about how you'd handle a small nightly file versus a firehose of events?

6Orchestration / Scheduling

How do you actually chain "ingest → clean → model → publish" into something that runs on a schedule, retries on failure, and tells someone when it breaks?

7Governance / Access Control

What's the real mechanism for controlling who can see which tables, or even which rows or columns, once the data is sitting in your lakehouse? Is "we'll lock it down later" ever actually an acceptable answer?

8Serving to a Business Stakeholder

Once your Gold table exists, how does an actual non-technical stakeholder get an answer out of it — a dashboard, a self-serve natural-language query, something else? What's the gap between "the table is correct" and "a stakeholder can use it"?

Be ready to explain, in your own words, what a service actually does. If you name a service in your pitch, someone in the room — a fellow learner or the facilitator — is allowed to ask "what does that actually do, and why not use X instead?" "It was on a slide somewhere" is not a defensible answer. Understand it well enough to explain it without reading off a page.

Same Bar For All 23 Pitches

What Every Pitch Must Cover

Every person gets one of four scenarios, but every pitch is judged against the same checklist, whether you're pitching QuickCart or FreshMart. This is also exactly what your Day8_Pitch_Template.html is structured to capture — fill in the template honestly and you'll naturally hit every item below.

Problem Statement A Scenario A

QuickCart Logistics — On-Demand Food Delivery

QuickCart started in one city, matching riders to nearby restaurants for same-hour delivery. Two years later, it operates in 40 cities — and growth has outpaced QuickCart's ability to agree, internally, on what's actually happening on any given day. Ops, finance, and product each pull their own numbers for the same day, and the three versions don't match. Meetings now spend more time arguing about whose number is right than deciding what to do about it.

The Problems

1Data Silos

Trip data lives in the app's own database. Merchant data lives in files. There's no shared way to query both together for one answer.

2No Real-Time Visibility

By the time anyone can compute "average delivery time by zone right now," the number is already stale.

3Inconsistent Merchant Performance View

Ops and merchant-partnerships each keep their own spreadsheet of "problem restaurants," and the two lists disagree.

4Unclear Data Freshness

Nobody can say, with confidence, how current a given dashboard number actually is.

Data Sources

  • 1A transactional database holding orders and trip_events — continuously updated throughout the day as a trip moves through assigned → picked_up → delivered.
  • 2Live rider GPS location pings — a high-frequency stream of location updates from the rider app, arriving continuously all day, every day.
  • 3A restaurant/merchant catalog — a nightly export file dropped into cloud storage by the merchant-onboarding system.

Business questions QuickCart currently can't answer: What is the average delivery time by zone, kept close to real time? Which restaurants have the highest order-cancellation rate this week? Which riders are under-utilized vs. over-loaded right now?

Problem Statement B Scenario B

MedTrack Clinics — Regional Healthcare Network

MedTrack runs 12 clinics across the region. Growth has meant more patients and more claims flowing through the business every month — and it's also brought a compliance team into the picture, now asking hard questions about who can actually see what. The systems that run the clinics were never designed with a shared answer to that question, and it's starting to matter.

The Problems

1Billing/Claims Don't Reconcile

What's recorded as visits doesn't match what the claims system says was actually billed.

2Real Compliance Exposure

Patient data currently has no consistent access-control model across teams. This is genuinely regulated data.

3Data Silos

Three systems, no shared source of truth between them.

4Slow, Manual Reporting

Compliance and audit reports are assembled by hand every quarter.

Data Sources

  • 1A transactional database holding patient_visits and diagnoses — continuously updated as visits happen.
  • 2Nightly lab-result files — an external partner lab drops result files once a night.
  • 3A third-party insurance-claims system reachable only through a live API call — MedTrack has to pull claim status updates from it, not a file drop.

Business questions MedTrack currently can't answer: What is the average cost per diagnosis category, and does it match what was actually billed? Which claims were denied last month, and why? Who accessed a given patient's record, and when?

Note if you drew Problem Statement B: governance and access control isn't optional here — make it central to your pitch, not a footnote at the end.

Problem Statement C Scenario C

StreamPlay Media — Subscription Video Streaming

StreamPlay has grown to millions of subscribers. Content and Product both claim ownership of "what makes people stay" — but every time either team presents retention numbers, they don't match the other team's version. Nobody fully trusts either one anymore, and decisions about what to renew or cancel are made on gut feel more often than anyone would like to admit.

The Problems

1No Consistent Retention Number

Content and Product compute "churn" differently and get different answers.

2Event Volume Overwhelms Ad-Hoc Queries

Nobody can query the raw event stream directly without it taking forever or falling over.

3Data Silos

Subscriber, event, and catalog data live in three unconnected systems.

4Slow Content Decisions

Deciding whether to renew a show's license takes weeks of manual data-pulling.

Data Sources

  • 1A transactional billing/subscriber database — continuously updated as people subscribe, cancel, or upgrade.
  • 2App clickstream events — a very high-volume stream of play/pause/skip/finish events, fired many times per second across every active user.
  • 3Content-catalog metadata — files describing each show/movie (genre, cast, release date), exported whenever the catalog changes.

Business questions StreamPlay currently can't answer: Which shows correlate with lower subscriber churn? What are daily/monthly active users by region? Which content is underperforming and should be reconsidered?

Note if you drew Problem Statement C: the sheer volume of the clickstream event source is the real design constraint here — a naive approach will not hold up, and that should show clearly in your pitch.

Problem Statement D Scenario D

FreshMart Grocery — In-Store + Online Retail

FreshMart runs 60 stores plus an online ordering channel. Leadership has one question that keeps coming up in every planning meeting: why do shelves go empty even when the warehouse system says there's stock? Nobody currently has the data connected in a way that can answer it.

The Problems

1Stockouts Don't Correlate With Anything

Nobody can tell if a stockout was a demand spike, a late delivery, or a faulty sensor.

2No Single Shrinkage/Waste Number

Every store reports waste differently. Some don't report it at all.

3Supplier Performance Is Invisible

No one can say which suppliers are reliably late or short-shipping.

4Messy, Inconsistent Source Files

Invoice formats vary so much between suppliers that some are hard to parse at all.

Data Sources

  • 1A transactional point-of-sale database — continuously updated at every checkout, in-store and online.
  • 2In-store IoT sensors — shelf-weight and fridge-temperature sensors in every store, emitting a steady stream of readings.
  • 3Supplier invoices — dropped as a messy mix of file formats (some clean spreadsheets, some barely machine-readable documents), format varies supplier to supplier, and arrival timing is irregular.

Business questions FreshMart currently can't answer: Which stores have the highest shrinkage/waste this month? Which suppliers have the most late or incomplete deliveries? Could shelf-sensor and point-of-sale data together predict a stockout before it happens?

Note if you drew Problem Statement D: don't wave the messy invoice files away — the pitch needs a real plan for actually handling them, not a hand-wave past the hard part.

Phase 2 ⏱ ~25 min build, 3 min pitch + 1 min Q&A

Build & Pitch

Once your own design is actually settled — not while you're still going back and forth with yourself about ingestion for Source 2 — copy Day8_Pitch_Template.html, rename it to something that identifies you and your problem statement, and fill it in with your own researched content. You'll present directly from that page, solo, to the whole class — the same way GlobalMart's own architecture was pitched to you on Day 1.

  • Copy Day8_Pitch_Template.html into your own working copy. Every CSS class in it is already styled — replace the REPLACE ME text and diagram nodes, don't invent new markup.
  • Fill in every section yourself: the story, the four problems, why you chose your platform, the diagram, the governance callout, and your fact table spotlight.
  • Make sure your filled-in diagram traces your actual chosen services end to end — source, ingestion, Bronze/Silver/Gold, orchestration, governance, serving.
  • Rehearse it once on your own before your slot — you're presenting solo, so you need to know the whole pitch, not just one part of it.

    • Pitch Format — 3 Minutes to Present, Up to 1 Minute of Q&A

    • Name the ingestion choice for each data source in your problem statement, and be ready to defend it if challenged.
    • Show the filled-in architecture diagram.
    • State your fact table's grain, measures, and dimensions out loud.
    • Name the one governance consideration you built in.
    • You're presenting to the whole class, not just your table — with 23 pitches back to back, keep it tight and stay inside the 3-minute mark so everyone gets their turn.
    Closing Note
